Mining research · Form 10-K · Historical filing

Newmont produced less gold in 2025 and sold more dollars of it. The 10-K explains how.

Newmont's 2025 annual report shows sales of $22,669 million, up from $18,682 million in 2024, and net income from continuing operations attributable to stockholders of $7,085 million, more than double the year before. The same report shows consolidated gold production falling from 6,545 thousand ounces to 5,530 thousand. The difference is price.

The headline and the ounces

Sales rose +21.3% and gold sales rose +22.6%. Consolidated gold production moved the other way, −15.5%. A reader who sees only the revenue line could assume the mines produced more. The filing shows they produced less.

The company's own explanation is in the discussion of results: the net increase in sales was “largely due to higher average realized gold prices partially offset by the impact from divestitures.” The report lists the sales: Telfer in the fourth quarter of 2024; CC&V, Musselwhite and Éléonore in the first quarter of 2025; Porcupine and Akyem in the second. Fewer mines produced fewer ounces.

Price did the work

The average realized gold price was $3,498 an ounce in 2025 against $2,408 in 2024, +45.3%. That is a number Newmont does not control. All-in sustaining cost, the non-GAAP measure Newmont reports on the World Gold Council definition, rose +6.1% to $1,609 an ounce. The margin per ounce widened because the price rose faster than the cost.

Item 1A of the same report lists the risk that runs the other way: gold, copper and other metal prices fluctuate daily and are affected by factors beyond the company's control.

What to read next in the filing

  • Item 1A, risk factors: commodity prices, reserves, operating and jurisdiction risks.
  • Proven and probable reserves: 118.2 million attributable ounces of gold at December 31, 2025, and how they compare with the year before.
  • The divestiture notes, to separate sites sold from performance at the sites kept.
  • The non-GAAP reconciliation, to see what is and is not inside all-in sustaining cost.

Questions to ask of any filing like this

  1. How much of the revenue change came from price, and how much from volume?
  2. Which mines were sold, and what did they produce before the sale?
  3. Are costs per ounce rising or falling, and on which measure?
  4. What does the company say could reverse the result?
