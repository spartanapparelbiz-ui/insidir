  1. Concept 01 · Insider transactions · EPD

    An insider purchase people misunderstood

    Hook · the first 1–3 seconds

    A co-CEO just bought $100,000 of his own partnership's units. Here's the line most people skip.

    Script · about 27 seconds, 67 words

    A co-CEO just bought $100,000 of his own partnership's units. Here's the line most people skip. This is the Form 4. Code P: a real purchase, 2,665 units. Now the ownership column: more than three million units he already holds. Real buying — and less than a tenth of one percent of what the form lists. The filing says what happened. Not why, and not what's next.

    On screen

    • The Form 4 on sec.gov: zoom on Table I, the code P and the ownership column
    • The public story page scrolling from the figures to “Read the ownership columns”
    • Product clip: Read insider trades (a real Form 4 line written out in plain words)

  2. Concept 02 · Insider transactions · EPD

    Why an insider purchase isn't automatically a buy signal

    Hook · the first 1–3 seconds

    “The CEO is buying” is not the same sentence as “the stock goes up.”

    Script · about 28 seconds, 71 words

    “The CEO is buying” is not the same sentence as “the stock goes up.” Here's how I check one. The code: P is a purchase; A, M, F and G are grants, option exercises, tax withholding and gifts. Direct or indirect: this one went into a trust. Size against what they already own. And what everyone else at the company filed that month. One filing is a fact, not a forecast.

    On screen

    • The Form 4 on sec.gov: the transaction code column and footnote 1
    • Product clip: Open anyone's file (the person's full record across filings)
    • The public story page: the four questions at the end

  3. Concept 03 · Mining research · NEM

    The details behind a gold-mining headline

    Hook · the first 1–3 seconds

    Newmont's profit more than doubled last year. Its mines produced less gold.

    Script · about 25 seconds, 62 words

    Newmont's profit more than doubled last year. Its mines produced less gold. Both are in the same 10-K. Net income from continuing operations: $7,085 million, against $3,280 million. Gold produced: 5,530 thousand ounces, down from 6,545 thousand. What changed? The average realized gold price went from $2,408 to $3,498 an ounce, and the company sold several mines. Same headline, very different story.

    On screen

    • The public story page: the six figures

  4. Concept 04 · Mining research · NEM

    Revenue growth versus underlying business performance

    Hook · the first 1–3 seconds

    Sales up 21%. Production down 15%. Both from the same annual report.

    Script · about 26 seconds, 64 words

    Sales up 21%. Production down 15%. Both from the same annual report. When revenue grows, ask one question: price or volume? For Newmont in 2025 it was price. Gold sold for 45% more per ounce, while cost per ounce rose about 6%. That widens the margin — and it's also the part management doesn't control. If the price turns, the same math runs backwards.

    On screen

    • The 10-K on sec.gov: sales by metal and ounces produced, side by side
    • The public story page: “Price did the work”
    • Product clip: Find any company (financials by year as the company filed them)

  5. Concept 05 · Company fundamentals · BRK.B

    Why Berkshire Hathaway can't be understood from one financial multiple

    Hook · the first 1–3 seconds

    Berkshire's earnings fell by a quarter last year. Most of that wasn't the businesses.

    Script · about 29 seconds, 73 words

    Berkshire's earnings fell by a quarter last year. Most of that wasn't the businesses. Net earnings: $66,968 million, down from $88,995 million. But the 10-K splits that total: insurance, a railroad, an energy company, manufacturing and retail — and investment gains. Take out the gains and a one-time impairment, and the operating lines fell about 6%. Berkshire itself calls the gains generally meaningless for judging one year. One multiple hides all of that.

    On screen

    • The 10-K on sec.gov: the table of net earnings by line in Management's Discussion and Analysis
    • The public story page: the line-by-line table
    • Product clip: Find any company (a company report and its filings)

  6. Concept 06 · All three filings

    Three things to examine when researching a public company

    Hook · the first 1–3 seconds

    Three checks I run before I believe any stock headline.

    Script · about 26 seconds, 66 words

    Three checks I run before I believe any stock headline. One: what kind of document is it? A Form 4 trade, an annual report, a press release — each proves something different. Two: what drove the number? Newmont's revenue grew on price, not ounces. Three: what's inside the total? Berkshire's earnings swing with its investments. Open the original filing every time. It's public, and it's free.

Keep it honest

What makes these videos trustworthy, and what Insidir will not partner on.

  • No calls to buy or sell.

    Explain what a filing says. Don't tell viewers what to do with their money, and don't predict prices.

  • Say it's historical.

    Name the filing and its date. These are past documents, not breaking news.

  • Public, not secret.

    Form 4s and annual reports are public SEC records. Never call them leaked, hidden or insider tips.

  • An insider purchase is not a promise.

    Prices can fall after insiders buy. Say what the filing proves, and what it doesn't.

  • Real screens only.

    Show the filing, the public pages and the product clips. No invented dashboards, accounts, returns or numbers.

  • No made-up testimonials.

    Speak as yourself. Don't present a paid partnership as an independent review or a customer story.
